Beyond Transformers: The Architecture of Mamba & State-Space Models

Elena Rostova, PhD
Systems & sequence modeling researcher

For the past seven years, the unquestioned architecture for nearly every sequential task has been attention. Transformers work — but their cost scales quadratically with sequence length, and that ceiling is becoming impossible to ignore as context windows grow.

State-space models take a different bet entirely. Instead of comparing every token to every other token, they compress history into a fixed-size hidden state and update it recurrently, giving some variants near-linear time and memory.

The core trade-off

Mamba's selective state-space mechanism lets the model choose, at each step, what to keep and what to forget from that compressed state — a departure from earlier linear-recurrence models that treated every token identically.

"The goal is not to abandon attention, but to compress context into continuous state representations."

In practice this trades some of attention's flexibility for dramatically better throughput on long sequences, particularly where the hardware bottleneck is memory bandwidth rather than raw compute.

Where it breaks down

Tasks requiring precise, long-range token-to-token comparison — like exact copying over very long spans — still favor attention. Selective SSMs approximate this well but not perfectly, which is why hybrid architectures are now the more active research direction.

Give me an example of when this actually breaks down
Precise long-range copying is the clearest failure case — e.g. reproducing an exact string from far earlier in a long document. The compressed hidden state can lose fine-grained token identity that full attention preserves directly.

Explain the selective mechanism simply
Think of it as the model deciding, word by word, "is this worth remembering?" — instead of carrying every past token forward equally, it updates a running summary and only keeps what matters for what comes next.
